What do you do?

A job title often decides how long people listen.
A friend reminded me it does not decide who you are.

BY SAURAV DAS·OCTOBER 24, 2026·10 MIN READ

01 / THE OBSERVATION

The question that sorts a room.

“What do you do?” sounds like small talk. Often it is a quiet way of deciding how much to listen.

It is usually the second question, right after your name. At a dinner, a conference, a wedding, a neighbor’s porch. I have asked it hundreds of times without thinking. Lately I have been paying attention to what happens after someone answers it.

The answer changes the room. A title that sounds senior gets follow-up questions and a little more patience. A title that sounds ordinary, or no title at all, often gets a polite nod and a change of subject. The same opinion about schools, or the economy, or what someone ought to do with their life, lands with a different weight depending on who it is attached to.

I have felt this from the inside. How much people want to hear from you, and how seriously they take what you say, can depend less on what you are saying than on the position you hold while you say it. Your job becomes a kind of volume knob, and other people have their hand on it.

Some of that is reasonable. None of us can check every claim ourselves, so we lean on signs of experience. I would rather hear about a broken bone from someone who has set one. But the shortcut rarely stops at expertise. It slides from “does this person know this subject?” to “is this person worth listening to at all?” Those are very different questions. A job title answers the first one only partly, and the second one not at all.

I have heard that second question answered out loud. More than once, and from more than one person, I have heard someone with a comfortable, well-paid job talk about the people at the bottom of the pay ladder, the cashier, the server, the driver, as if their opinions did not count. Some version of: they don’t know anything; they barely make minimum wage; I don’t need to hear from them. It is usually said casually, which is what makes it so odd. Nobody in those conversations was talking about expertise. A wage had become a measure of whether a person was worth hearing.

This issue is about that slide, and about a conversation with a friend that showed me what it looks like from the other side.

02 / THE CONVERSATION

“I have always been a scientist.”

A friend of mine lost her job almost a year ago. She is a scientist. Since then she has been applying, interviewing, waiting, and applying again. When she told me how hard the search had become, I did what I think many of us would do. I tried to fix it.

I suggested she pick up something part-time while she looked. Drive for Uber or Lyft, take a few shifts somewhere, anything to keep money coming in and the weeks moving. It felt like practical, caring advice. Rent does not wait for the right job.

Her answer has stayed with me. This is how I remember it:

“I have always been a scientist. I want to be a scientist and an academic, even if I have to struggle for it.”A FRIEND · AS I REMEMBER IT, NOT A RECORDING

In that moment I understood that we had been having two different conversations. I was talking about income. She was talking about who she is. My suggestion treated her job as a paycheck that another paycheck could replace. To her, “scientist” was not a line on a résumé she could set aside for a while. It was the name she has for herself.

I do not think my advice was wrong, exactly. Money matters, and plenty of scientists, artists, and teachers have driven, waited tables, or worked nights to keep doing the thing they are. But I offered it in the wrong order. I answered a question about getting by before I had listened to a question about identity.

Later, something else struck me. The work I had suggested is exactly the kind of work I have heard people dismiss. There is nothing wrong with that work. The problem is the listeners who stop listening to whoever does it. I had been offering my friend a paycheck without thinking about the hearing that might come with it.

What struck me most was how firmly she held on. The world had taken the job. It had not taken the word. Even while struggling, she would not let the search rename her.

03 / A LENS

What a job gives besides money.

An old study helped me make sense of that conversation. In 1930, the textile factory in Marienthal, a workers’ settlement outside Vienna, closed during the global economic crisis. From November 1931 to May 1932, a team of researchers including Marie Jahoda, Paul Lazarsfeld, and Hans Zeisel studied what long unemployment was doing to the community. [1]

Many people at the time expected the unemployed to become angry, even revolutionary. What the team described instead was a tired community, marked by resignation. Losing work had taken more than wages. [1]

Jahoda spent much of her later career on that observation. She argued that a job has one obvious function, income, and several hidden ones that we notice only when they disappear: a structure for the day, contact with people outside the family, a sense of being part of something larger, regular activity, and status, which she tied closely to identity. [2]

That list describes my conversation almost exactly. I had offered my friend the obvious function. A part-time gig can replace some income. She was talking about the last item on Jahoda’s list: the part of a job that tells you, and everyone around you, who you are.

A 2023 meta-analysis put numbers on the idea. Karsten Paul and colleagues pooled studies that compared employed and unemployed people on each of these functions. Every difference pointed the same way, but the largest one was not money. It was status. [3]

The authors define status as the experience of being valued and appreciated by the people around you and by society at large. Read that way, the chart joins the two halves of this issue. If people decide how much to listen based on your job, then losing the job does not only cost a salary. It can cost you the hearing.

Seen like that, my friend’s insistence on calling herself a scientist looks less like stubbornness and more like a refusal to let the second loss follow the first.

The chart has limits. These are averages across many people and places, the status estimate has a wide range, and a pooled number cannot tell anyone how a particular person feels. But it put one small conversation into a larger pattern. Some of what hurts about losing work is what other people stop seeing.

04 / THE OTHER LABEL

Heard before you speak.

A job title is at least a label you can change. Some labels walk into the room before you say a word.

In my own life, I have felt that how much people want to listen to me, and how much respect they offer, can depend on the color of my skin. It is not always dramatic. Often it is a matter of attention: how long someone listens, how much benefit of the doubt they give, how much proof they need first. And it is serious, in more areas of life than I would like.

I want to be careful here. I cannot see inside anyone’s head, and not every cool reception is about race. But I have felt it often enough, and in enough different places, to take it seriously.

Researchers have measured one version of this. In a field experiment published in 2004, Marianne Bertrand and Sendhil Mullainathan answered job advertisements in Boston and Chicago with fictitious résumés, randomly giving them names that signaled race. Résumés with white-sounding names received about 50 percent more callbacks than those with Black-sounding names. Because the names were assigned at random, the gap could not come from the résumés themselves. [4]

That study is about hiring in the United States more than twenty years ago, not about me. I bring it in for one reason. It shows, in numbers, the thing I have felt in person: a label can be judged before the work is read. Someone decides how much to listen before the listening starts.

The job title and the skin color work the same way in one respect. Both let a person decide your weight without hearing you out. The difference is that you can lose one in a single meeting, and the other comes into every room with you.

05 / PROPORTION

My five percent.

I need to say this clearly, because writing about the hard part can make it sound like the whole story. It is not. This is maybe five percent of my experience.

The other ninety-five percent is people. I am so blessed to have so many good friends and so much family who listen to me as a person, not as a position. They ask how I am before they ask what I do.

I write about the five percent because it is easy to miss from inside the ninety-five. When you are surrounded by people who see you, you can forget how many rooms decide first and listen later. And for some people, including a friend in the middle of a long job search, the proportions run the other way for a while.

The ninety-five percent is also the answer. The people who make it up show what it looks like to give someone their full weight, whatever their title. That is something any of us can practice.

06 / RETURN TO THE QUESTION

A better first question.

If I could have that conversation with my friend again, I would still care about the rent. But I would start somewhere else. I would ask what kind of science she wants to do next, what she is curious about right now, and how I could help her keep doing it while she searches. The practical help could come after that, and it would land differently, because it would be help for a scientist, not a replacement for one.

I want to try something smaller in ordinary life, too. When I meet someone new, I want to wait before asking what they do. There are better first questions. What are you curious about these days? What has been on your mind? What are you working toward? A job is one answer to those questions. It is not the only one, and it is not the person.

A title can open a door. It should not decide who is worth hearing once you are inside.
